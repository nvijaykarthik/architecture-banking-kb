## 1. Precise definition
Cash management is the institutional process of pooling cash from multiple client accounts into a master account or money-market vehicle, applying investment mandates, and monitoring for yield, liquidity, and regulatory compliance (ESMA/MiFID, CSDR, FIR). It is a fiduciary optimization problem: maximize client yield and bank funding efficiency, while tracking individual client entitlements.

## 2. Why this exists (the problem it solves)
Before cash management, idle cash sat in individual client accounts earning zero or negligible interest. The 2008 crisis taught banks that idle cash is a funding drain and a competitive disadvantage. CSDR and MiFID II did not forbid pooling but required "identification of beneficial ownership at all times." The challenge: design a system that is economically efficient (one master account, one interest line) but legally segregated (each client's proportional share is known and tracked).

## 3. Core concepts & vocabulary
| Term | Precise meaning |
|------|-----------------|
| Cash pooling | Consolidating cash from multiple accounts into a master account or vehicle to maximize interest yield or minimize loan size |
| Cash sweep | Automated movement of idle cash from a client account to a master account or money-market fund by a pre-defined threshold and frequency |
| Cash master account | A bank-owned or multi-party pooled account that holds aggregated cash; not a client account; used for yield optimization and liquidity |
| Reinvestment | Placement of swept or unclaimed cash into a yield-generating instrument (term account, securities lending, repo, money-market fund) |
| Unclaimed cash / escheat | Cash in a client account that has been idle for a statutory period (e.g., 3–5 years) and legally or contractually reverts to the bank |
| ESMA MAR / MiFID II | EU restrictions on bank incentives for sweep-into-fund arrangements; own-account trading privileges require disclosure |
| CSDR | EU regulation requiring segregation of client assets; client cash can be pooled for investment, but ownership must be individually identifiable at all times |
| Arbitrage risk | The risk that cash swept into a low-yield instrument could have been lent at a higher rate or reinvested at a higher yield |
| Liquidity | The ability to meet cash calls and collateral calls on demand, without invoking liquidation
| Yield | The return generated by cash management; target is usually above the bank's cost of funds and economically defensible
| Reinvestment mandate | The pre-defined rules and instruments for placing swept cash; approved by compliance, risk, and governance
| Holdback | A percentage of cash that is intentionally retained by the bank to cover settlement obligations, tax, or fees

## 4. How it works (architecture / mechanism)
Cash management follows a typical program flow:
1. **Cash pool / sweep event:** The system monitors all client cash accounts; if the balance exceeds a daily threshold (or pool has accumulated to a set level), a sweep is triggered.
2. **Sweep validation:** Compliance and risk review the pool for adequacy (IRR > target, credit-quality filters).
3. **Investment:** The pooled cash is placed into a designated money-market vehicle or term account.
4. **Portfolio management:** The fund manager may trade within a rated universe; the system tracks per-client allocation through an internal sub-account ledger.
5. **Interest:** Interest or coupon is accrued and credited back to each client proportionally.
6. **Rebalancing:** If one client withdraws a large amount, the portfolio must be rebalanced or a portion liquidated.
7. **Unclaimed cash:** After a statutory period (e.g., 3 years), unclaimed cash is reverted to the bank in accordance with local escheat rules.

## 5. Variants, options & trade-offs
| Variant | When to pick | When to avoid | Key trade-off axis |
|---------|--------------|---------------|--------------------|
| Master account pooling | Large-scale retail, high-volume cash, or multi-currency for-king | Client mandates prohibit pooling | Yield vs. regulatory defense |
| Sweep-into-fund | When ESMA/MiFID and fund-related rules permit and regulatory requirements are met | When the fund is not rated or is illiquid | Yield vs. regulatory compliance |
| Sweep-into-term-account | Simpler, lower yield, but easier to attribute per client | When yield optimization is required | Compliance vs. optimization |
| Reverse repo / securities lending | When the bank has excess securities to lend | When the client has a debit or limited securities basket | Yield vs. credit |
| Holdback | When the bank requires a buffer for settlement or fees | When the bank is over-collateralized and holdback is economically inefficient | Funding cost vs. flexibility |

- **C3-11 (ESMA directives):** ESMA/MiFID rules on fund incentives and disclosure apply to sweep-into-fund arrangements.

## 7. Banking / financial-services context 💳
In 2023, a European bank's master account-based cash sweeping was challenged by a client who had ceased operations 4 years prior; the unclaimed cash had reverted to the bank per local escheat rules, but the client's legal heir claimed the cash. The dispute required 2 years of probate; the bank lost interest and incurred legal fees. The lesson: an internal escheat policy is not a substitute for a living client; the architect must design an alert for post-mortem accounts.

## 8. Reference architecture / worked example
**Problem:** A US broker-dealer wants to aggregate ~$800m in retail cash for reinvestment in a money-market fund.
**Decision:** Build a master account with per-client sub-ledger, sweep daily, and use a MiFID II-compliant third-party fund; obtain written consent from each client; document the compliance review.
**ADR:**
```markdown
# ADR-06: Sweep-into-Fund for Retail Cash
## Status
Accepted
## Context
Onboard a US broker-dealer with $800m retail cash sweep into a money-market fund; MiFID II disclosure and ESMA incentive rules apply.
## Decision
Use a MiFID II-compliant third-party fund; present a clear, un-bundled fee structure; implement a per-client sub-ledger; obtain e-signature consent.
## Consequences
- + Yield optimization; lower funding costs
- - Compliance burden; incentive-disclosure requirements
- - Post-mortem escheat risk
## Alternatives
1. Master account with term-account (rejected: lower yield)
2. Direct floor-transfer (rejected: costly)
```

## 9. Maturity & adoption signals
- **Adopt when:** cash > 5% of total assets, interest earnings > €0.5m/year, or client demand for sweep-into-fund
- **Anti-signals:** > 3 years unclaimed per-client; > 20% of assets in non-liquid sweep vehicles; sweep-rate below benchmark for > 6 months
- **Common failure modes:** (1) unclaimed cash escheat delay; (2) incentive misalignment (Esma-d).

## 10. Common confusions — the "don't mix" list
| Often confused | Real distinction |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Cash pooling vs cash sweep | Pooling = consolidation; sweep = automated movement |
| Cash management vs treasury funding | Cash management serves clients; treasury funding serves the bank |
| Reinvestment vs reconveyance | Reinvestment generates yield; reconveyance returns cash |
| Master account vs client account | Master account is pooled and bank-owned; client account is individually owned |

## Summary
Cash management is a fiduciary optimization problem: maximize client yield and bank funding efficiency, while tracking individual client entitlements. The tension is between pooling efficiency (master account, one yield line) and regulatory segregation (individual tracking). The architect must design a "glass-box" system: one master account with a sub-ledger; one yield to split; one compliance-gated investment; and one unclaimed-cash reversion path.
